# Solar System — SpaceEngine Visual Atlas

A guided catalogue of the Solar System scenes in this folder. Each entry combines a description of the image with a compact set of scientific facts and a note on why the object matters.

> **Image and scale note:** These images were rendered with SpaceEngine. They are visualizations, not necessarily photographs or spacecraft views from the exact positions shown. Apparent sizes, distances, lighting, colours, and backgrounds may be artistically or computationally represented; do not measure physical scale from the pictures unless the scene explicitly provides a scale.

## At a glance

The Solar System formed about 4.6 billion years ago from a collapsing cloud of gas and dust. It contains the Sun, eight planets, dwarf planets, moons, rings, asteroids, comets, and small icy bodies. The four inner planets are rocky; Jupiter and Saturn are gas giants; Uranus and Neptune are ice giants. Beyond Neptune lies the Kuiper Belt, home to Pluto, Haumea, Makemake, and many smaller icy worlds.

Values below are rounded reference values. “Orbit” means the time to orbit the named parent body for a moon, or the Sun for a planet/dwarf planet. For the Sun and ring-particle scenes, an orbital period is not applicable in the same way. Physical properties of small, distant objects may be uncertain or inferred.



## Callisto — Jupiter’s ancient, cratered moon

![Callisto — Jupiter’s ancient, cratered moon](<Callisto.jpg>)

Callisto is an icy-rocky satellite with one of the most heavily cratered surfaces in the Solar System. Its battered landscape preserves a record of impacts over billions of years. A subsurface ocean is possible, though less directly supported than Europa’s.

**Key facts:** **Mass:** 1.075 × 10²³ kg · **Mean diameter:** 4,821 km · **Mean density:** 1.83 g/cm³ · **Surface gravity:** 1.24 m/s². **Orbit:** 16.69 Earth days around Jupiter, at a mean distance of about 1.88 million km. It is tidally locked.

**Why it matters:** Callisto’s relatively low density indicates abundant ice mixed with rock. It has an extremely thin carbon-dioxide-rich exosphere, not a breathable atmosphere. Its old surface and comparatively weak tidal heating make it a useful contrast with Io and Europa.


## Earth — the ocean world

![Earth — the ocean world](<Earth.jpg>)

Earth is the only world currently known to host life. Liquid water covers about 71% of its surface, while its atmosphere and magnetic field help maintain a habitable environment.

**Key facts:** **Mass:** 5.972 × 10²⁴ kg · **Mean diameter:** 12,742 km · **Mean density:** 5.51 g/cm³ · **Surface gravity:** 9.81 m/s². **Solar orbit:** 365.256 days at 1 AU (149.6 million km). **Sidereal rotation:** about 23 h 56 min.

**Why it matters:** Earth is differentiated into an iron-nickel core, rocky mantle, and crust. Its nitrogen–oxygen atmosphere, active water cycle, plate tectonics, and global magnetic field are key features. The image is a SpaceEngine rendering rather than a photograph of Earth from this exact viewpoint.


## Earth and Moon — a two-body system

![Earth and Moon — a two-body system](<Earthand%20Moon.jpg>)

This view emphasizes the Moon’s size relative to Earth. The Moon is unusually large compared with its host planet and strongly influences tides and Earth’s axial stability.

**Key facts:** **Earth mass:** 5.972 × 10²⁴ kg; **Moon mass:** 7.342 × 10²² kg. **Moon diameter:** 3,475 km. **Mean Earth–Moon distance:** 384,400 km. **Lunar orbit:** 27.32 days relative to the stars; phases repeat every 29.53 days.

**Why it matters:** The Moon is tidally locked, so nearly the same hemisphere faces Earth. Its surface is silicate rock with ancient highlands, basaltic maria, and impact craters. The rendering may not use true scale for distance or illumination.


## The Moon beside Earth

![The Moon beside Earth](<EarthMoon.jpg>)

A second Earth–Moon composition highlights the Moon’s cratered, airless surface and the contrast between Earth’s active, water-rich surface and the Moon’s preserved impact record.

**Key facts:** **Moon mass:** 7.342 × 10²² kg · **Mean diameter:** 3,475 km · **Surface gravity:** 1.62 m/s². **Orbit around Earth:** 27.32 days (sidereal). The Moon also orbits the Sun together with Earth.

**Why it matters:** The Moon has no substantial atmosphere, so there is little weathering by wind or liquid water. Lunar samples and orbital mapping support a history that includes a giant impact, magma-ocean cooling, volcanism, and heavy bombardment.


## Enceladus — an active ocean moon

![Enceladus — an active ocean moon](<Enceladus.jpg>)

Enceladus is a small, bright, icy moon of Saturn. South-polar fractures known as tiger stripes vent water-rich plumes into space, providing direct samples of material from a global subsurface ocean.

**Key facts:** **Mass:** 1.08 × 10²⁰ kg · **Mean diameter:** 504 km · **Mean density:** 1.61 g/cm³ · **Surface gravity:** about 0.11 m/s². **Orbit:** 1.37 days around Saturn.

**Why it matters:** Its surface is dominated by water ice. Cassini detected water vapour, salts, organic compounds, and molecular hydrogen in plume material; these observations make Enceladus a major astrobiology target, but do not demonstrate that life exists there.


## Europa — an ice shell over a global ocean?

![Europa — an ice shell over a global ocean?](<Europa.jpg>)

Europa’s relatively smooth, fractured ice surface suggests a geologically active world. Strong evidence indicates a salty liquid-water ocean beneath its ice shell.

**Key facts:** **Mass:** 4.80 × 10²² kg · **Mean diameter:** 3,122 km · **Mean density:** 3.01 g/cm³ · **Surface gravity:** 1.31 m/s². **Orbit:** 3.55 days around Jupiter.

**Why it matters:** Europa is made of a rocky interior beneath an ice shell and likely a global ocean. Jupiter’s tides flex Europa and supply internal heat. NASA’s Europa Clipper mission is designed to assess habitability, not to detect life directly.


## Haumea — a rapidly rotating dwarf planet

![Haumea — a rapidly rotating dwarf planet](<Haumea1.jpg>)

Haumea is an elongated, fast-spinning dwarf planet in the Kuiper Belt beyond Neptune. Its rapid rotation distorts it from a sphere, and it is known to possess a ring.

**Key facts:** **Mass:** approximately 4.0 × 10²¹ kg · **Approximate longest dimension:** around 2,300 km (shape-dependent) · **Solar orbit:** about 284 Earth years · **Rotation:** about 3.9 hours.

**Why it matters:** Haumea is thought to consist largely of rock with a surface coating of water ice. It has two known moons, Hiʻiaka and Namaka. Its rapid spin and a family of icy fragments may preserve evidence of an ancient collision.


## Haumea viewed with its moon Namaka

![Haumea viewed with its moon Namaka](<HaumeafromNamaka.jpg>)

This simulated viewpoint presents Haumea with one of its two known moons, Namaka. The apparent spacing and sizes in a simulation should not be interpreted as a scale model.

**Key facts:** **Haumea:** mass ≈ 4.0 × 10²¹ kg; solar period ≈ 284 years. **Namaka:** small outer-system satellite with an orbital period of roughly 18 days (the value is approximate and orbital solutions can be refined).

**Why it matters:** Haumea’s moons are faint and difficult to observe from Earth. Their orbits help constrain Haumea’s mass and history. The scene is a visualization, not a direct photograph from a spacecraft.


## Hiʻiaka and Haumea

![Hiʻiaka and Haumea](<HriakaHaumea.jpg>)

The filename appears to refer to Hiʻiaka, Haumea’s larger known moon. The view illustrates a distant dwarf-planet system rather than a spacecraft photograph.

**Key facts:** **Haumea:** solar orbital period ≈ 284 years; rotation ≈ 3.9 hours. **Hiʻiaka:** its orbit around Haumea takes about 49 days. The moons are much smaller than Haumea and are not well characterized in bulk properties.

**Why it matters:** Hiʻiaka’s spectrum shows water ice. Studying the moons and related Kuiper Belt fragments helps test whether a collision stripped ice and spun Haumea up in the early Solar System.

## Io — the volcanically active moon

![Io — the volcanically active moon](<Io.jpg>)

Io’s yellow, orange, and brown surface is coated by sulfur compounds and volcanic deposits. It is the most volcanically active body known in the Solar System.

**Key facts:** **Mass:** 8.93 × 10²² kg · **Mean diameter:** 3,643 km · **Mean density:** 3.53 g/cm³ · **Surface gravity:** 1.80 m/s². **Orbit:** 1.77 days around Jupiter.

**Why it matters:** Io is rocky and has little water ice. Gravitational tugs from Jupiter and orbital resonances with Europa and Ganymede flex its interior, generating heat that powers hundreds of volcanoes and extensive lava flows.


## Jupiter — the giant planet

![Jupiter — the giant planet](<Jupiter.jpg>)

Jupiter is the Solar System’s most massive planet. Its alternating cloud bands, storms, and Great Red Spot reveal a dynamic atmosphere driven by internal heat and rapid rotation.

**Key facts:** **Mass:** 1.898 × 10²⁷ kg (about 318 Earth masses) · **Equatorial diameter:** about 143,000 km · **Mean density:** 1.33 g/cm³. **Solar orbit:** 11.86 years · **Rotation:** about 9.9 hours.

**Why it matters:** Jupiter is primarily hydrogen and helium, with no solid surface to stand on. Pressure increases inward to a likely metallic-hydrogen region around a heavy-element-rich interior. Its powerful magnetosphere and extensive moon system make it a planetary system in miniature.


## Jupiter’s Galilean moons

![Jupiter’s Galilean moons](<JupiterIoCallistoEuropa.jpg>)

The scene shows Jupiter’s large moons Io, Europa, and Callisto. Along with Ganymede (not necessarily visible here), these are the four Galilean satellites discovered by Galileo in 1610.

**Key facts:** **Orbital periods:** Io 1.77 days; Europa 3.55 days; Ganymede 7.15 days; Callisto 16.69 days. **Diameters:** Io 3,643 km; Europa 3,122 km; Ganymede 5,268 km; Callisto 4,821 km.

**Why it matters:** The first three moons participate in a 4:2:1 orbital resonance. Their different compositions and geological states—from Io’s volcanism to Europa’s ocean and Callisto’s ancient craters—make them a comparative laboratory for icy and rocky worlds.


## Jupiter’s faint ring system

![Jupiter’s faint ring system](<JupiterRings.jpg>)

Jupiter has a tenuous ring system made mainly of dark dust, much fainter than Saturn’s rings. Dust from tiny inner moons is lofted into orbit by impacts and contributes to the rings.

**Key facts:** **Host planet:** Jupiter has a mass of 1.898 × 10²⁷ kg and orbits the Sun every 11.86 years. The rings consist of particles on many orbits close to Jupiter rather than a single solid structure.

**Why it matters:** Jupiter’s main ring is narrow and dusty; fainter halo and gossamer rings extend farther out. The rings are difficult to see from Earth and were discovered by Voyager 1 in 1979. This is a simulation of the ring system.


## Makemake — a Kuiper Belt dwarf planet

![Makemake — a Kuiper Belt dwarf planet](<Makemake.jpg>)

Makemake is a cold, distant dwarf planet in the Kuiper Belt. Its surface reflects sunlight strongly and is coated in volatile ices.

**Key facts:** **Mass:** about 3.1 × 10²¹ kg (estimated) · **Diameter:** roughly 1,430 km · **Solar orbit:** about 306 Earth years · **Mean solar distance:** around 45 AU.

**Why it matters:** Spectroscopy indicates methane and other frozen compounds at the surface. Makemake has one known moon, provisionally designated MK2. Its distant orbit and small size make detailed surface study challenging.


## Mars — the red planet

![Mars — the red planet](<Mars.jpg>)

Mars is a cold desert world with giant volcanoes, ancient river valleys, polar caps, and evidence that liquid water once flowed across its surface.

**Key facts:** **Mass:** 6.42 × 10²³ kg (0.107 Earth masses) · **Mean diameter:** 6,779 km · **Surface gravity:** 3.71 m/s². **Solar orbit:** 687 Earth days · **Rotation:** 24 h 37 min.

**Why it matters:** Mars is a rocky planet with a thin atmosphere dominated by carbon dioxide. Iron-bearing minerals oxidize at the surface and contribute to its red colour. Orbital and rover missions investigate its climate history and whether past environments could have supported microbial life.


## Mars viewed from Phobos

![Mars viewed from Phobos](<MarsfromPhobos%20(1).jpg>)

Phobos is the larger and closer of Mars’s two small moons. From its surface, Mars would appear enormous compared with the Moon as seen from Earth; the exact scale and perspective here are simulated.

**Key facts:** **Mars:** solar year 687 Earth days. **Phobos:** mean diameter about 22 km; mass ≈ 1.07 × 10¹⁶ kg; orbital period ≈ 7 h 39 min, faster than Mars rotates.

**Why it matters:** Phobos is irregular, heavily cratered, and likely a rubble-rich body. Its orbit is slowly decaying; models predict it will eventually break up into a ring or impact Mars on a timescale of tens of millions of years.


## Mars and its polar region

![Mars and its polar region](<MarsPole.jpg>)

The image highlights Mars’s polar terrain. Mars has seasonal polar caps made of water ice and carbon-dioxide ice, with layered deposits that preserve clues about past climate cycles.

**Key facts:** **Mars:** mass 6.42 × 10²³ kg; mean diameter 6,779 km; solar orbital period 687 Earth days. A Martian day (sol) is about 24 h 39 min.

**Why it matters:** The permanent residual north polar cap is mostly water ice; seasonal carbon-dioxide frost grows and retreats with the seasons. Dust, wind, and sublimation continually reshape the polar environment.


## Mercury — the innermost planet

![Mercury — the innermost planet](<Mercury.jpg>)

Mercury is the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun. It has an ancient, heavily cratered surface and extreme day–night temperature contrasts.

**Key facts:** **Mass:** 3.30 × 10²³ kg · **Mean diameter:** 4,879 km · **Mean density:** 5.43 g/cm³ · **Surface gravity:** 3.70 m/s². **Solar orbit:** 88 Earth days · **Rotation:** 58.6 Earth days.

**Why it matters:** Mercury has a disproportionately large iron-rich core, a thin exosphere rather than a substantial atmosphere, and water ice in permanently shadowed polar craters. Its 3:2 spin-orbit resonance means it rotates three times for every two solar orbits.


## Mercury against the starfield

![Mercury against the starfield](<Mercury1.jpg>)

This wider simulated view places Mercury in its solar-system context. Mercury’s phase changes as it orbits the Sun, just as Venus’s does, because we see different portions of its sunlit hemisphere.

**Key facts:** **Mercury:** mass 3.30 × 10²³ kg; diameter 4,879 km; orbital period 88 days; average distance 0.387 AU from the Sun.

**Why it matters:** Mercury’s orbit is eccentric and its perihelion precesses. The planet has a global magnetic field, much weaker than Earth’s, which interacts with the solar wind.


## Neptune — a distant ice giant

![Neptune — a distant ice giant](<Neptune.jpg>)

Neptune is a deep-blue ice giant with fast winds, storms, and a faint ring system. Its blue appearance is influenced by atmospheric methane and other atmospheric processes; colour alone does not reveal its full composition.

**Key facts:** **Mass:** 1.024 × 10²⁶ kg (about 17 Earth masses) · **Mean diameter:** 49,244 km · **Mean density:** 1.64 g/cm³. **Solar orbit:** 164.8 years · **Rotation:** about 16.1 hours.

**Why it matters:** Neptune’s atmosphere is mostly hydrogen and helium with methane; its interior contains water-, ammonia-, and methane-rich material around a rocky core. It was predicted mathematically before telescopic observation confirmed it in 1846.


## Neptune from Triton

![Neptune from Triton](<NeptunefromTriton.jpg>)

Triton is Neptune’s largest moon. The simulated view shows Neptune rising over Triton’s horizon; it is not an actual photograph taken from the surface.

**Key facts:** **Triton:** diameter about 2,710 km; mass ≈ 2.14 × 10²² kg; orbital period ≈ 5.88 days, in a retrograde orbit around Neptune. **Neptune:** solar year ≈ 165 Earth years.

**Why it matters:** Triton’s retrograde orbit suggests it was captured from the Kuiper Belt. It has a nitrogen-rich tenuous atmosphere, an icy surface, and evidence of cryovolcanic activity. Tidal evolution may eventually cause Triton to break apart or collide with Neptune.


## Pluto — a complex dwarf planet

![Pluto — a complex dwarf planet](<Pluto.jpg>)

Pluto is a Kuiper Belt dwarf planet with mountains, glaciers, impact craters, and a thin atmosphere. NASA’s New Horizons flyby in 2015 transformed it from a point of light into a geologically diverse world.

**Key facts:** **Mass:** 1.303 × 10²² kg · **Mean diameter:** 2,377 km · **Mean density:** about 1.85 g/cm³. **Solar orbit:** 248 years · **Rotation:** 6.39 Earth days.

**Why it matters:** Pluto likely has a rocky interior and water-ice crust, with surface ices of nitrogen, methane, and carbon monoxide. Its large moon Charon is so substantial that Pluto and Charon orbit a shared centre outside Pluto’s body.


## Close-up of Saturn’s ring particles

![Close-up of Saturn’s ring particles](<SaturnRIngRocks.jpg>)

Saturn’s rings are not solid discs: they consist of countless particles, mostly water ice with a range of sizes from dust to boulder-scale chunks. Gaps and waves arise through gravitational interactions.

**Key facts:** **Ring particles:** orbit Saturn at different distances and therefore have different orbital periods; closer particles move faster. Saturn’s own solar orbital period is about 29.4 Earth years.

**Why it matters:** The rings are extremely thin relative to their width. Embedded moons, resonances, and collisions sculpt ring structure. The image is a simulated close-up, not a photograph of one specific particle field at a human-observable scale.


## Saturn’s icy rings from another angle

![Saturn’s icy rings from another angle](<SaturnRingRocks2.jpg>)

This view emphasizes the three-dimensional geometry of ring particles and the way sunlight scatters from icy material. The bright appearance depends on viewing angle and illumination.

**Key facts:** **Saturn:** mass 5.68 × 10²⁶ kg; mean radius about 58,232 km; solar orbital period 29.45 years. Ring particles range from fine grains to much larger icy fragments.

**Why it matters:** The main rings extend roughly 70,000 km outward from Saturn but are only tens of metres thick in many regions. Their age remains an active research question; Cassini measurements indicate ring material is gradually being lost into the planet.


## Saturn’s rings and shadow

![Saturn’s rings and shadow](<SaturnRings.jpg>)

Saturn’s spectacular ring system is composed mainly of water ice. The apparent dark gap between the bright A and B rings is the Cassini Division, a region shaped in part by orbital resonances with moons.

**Key facts:** **Saturn:** mass 5.68 × 10²⁶ kg; equatorial diameter about 120,536 km; rotation roughly 10.7 hours; solar orbit 29.45 years.

**Why it matters:** Saturn is a hydrogen–helium gas giant with no solid surface. Its rings span hundreds of thousands of kilometres but are astonishingly thin. The rings are likely transient on astronomical timescales, although their precise origin and age are debated.


## Saturn — the ringed giant

![Saturn — the ringed giant](<Staurn.jpg>)

Saturn is a low-density gas giant best known for its extensive rings. Its atmosphere contains bands, storms, and a persistent north-polar hexagonal jet-stream pattern.

**Key facts:** **Mass:** 5.68 × 10²⁶ kg (95 Earth masses) · **Equatorial diameter:** about 120,536 km · **Mean density:** 0.69 g/cm³. **Solar orbit:** 29.45 years · **Rotation:** approximately 10.7 hours.

**Why it matters:** Saturn is mostly hydrogen and helium. Its mean density is lower than water’s, though this does not mean the planet could practically float in a bathtub: it has no solid surface and would be subject to enormous pressure. Its moons include Titan and ocean-bearing Enceladus.


## The Sun — the star at the centre

![The Sun — the star at the centre](<Sun.jpg>)

The Sun is a G-type main-sequence star whose gravity binds the Solar System. Nuclear fusion in its core converts hydrogen into helium and releases the energy that powers sunlight and the solar wind.

**Key facts:** **Mass:** 1.989 × 10³⁰ kg (about 333,000 Earth masses) · **Diameter:** about 1.392 million km · **Photosphere temperature:** about 5,500 °C · **Core temperature:** about 15 million °C. **Rotation:** roughly 25 days at the equator, slower near the poles.

**Why it matters:** The Sun is composed primarily of hydrogen and helium. It formed about 4.6 billion years ago and is roughly halfway through its main-sequence lifetime. Solar flares and coronal mass ejections can disturb space weather and affect technology around Earth.


## The Sun as seen from Jupiter

![The Sun as seen from Jupiter](<SunfromJupiter.jpg>)

At Jupiter’s distance, the Sun appears smaller than it does from Earth and provides less than four percent of the sunlight received at Earth. The scene is a simulated view from a hypothetical viewpoint.

**Key facts:** **Jupiter’s mean solar distance:** 5.2 AU · **Solar orbital period:** 11.86 years. Sunlight takes about 43 minutes to reach Jupiter, compared with about 8 minutes 20 seconds to reach Earth.

**Why it matters:** Solar illumination decreases approximately with the square of distance. Jupiter’s moons still receive sunlight, but their environments are also strongly influenced by Jupiter’s magnetosphere and, for inner moons, tidal heating.


## Solar-system orbits — a schematic view

![Solar-system orbits — a schematic view](<Sun_Orbits%20(1).jpg>)

This image visualizes orbital paths around the Sun. The planets orbit in broadly the same direction and in nearly the same plane because they formed from a rotating protoplanetary disk. Real orbits are not luminous green lines.

**Key facts:** **Reference scale:** Earth’s mean orbital distance is 1 astronomical unit (AU), about 149.6 million km. Orbital periods range from 88 days for Mercury to about 165 years for Neptune; Pluto takes about 248 years.

**Why it matters:** Planetary orbits are ellipses, not perfect circles. The apparent spacing, line brightness, and perspective in this image are simulation choices; they should not be used as a quantitative plot unless the visualization explicitly states its scale.


## Titan — Saturn’s haze-covered moon

![Titan — Saturn’s haze-covered moon](<Titan.jpg>)

Titan is Saturn’s largest moon and the only moon with a dense atmosphere. Its surface has rivers, lakes, and seas of liquid methane and ethane, along with dunes made of organic-rich material.

**Key facts:** **Mass:** 1.345 × 10²³ kg · **Mean diameter:** 5,150 km · **Mean density:** 1.88 g/cm³ · **Surface gravity:** 1.35 m/s². **Orbit:** 15.95 days around Saturn.

**Why it matters:** Titan’s atmosphere is mostly nitrogen with methane and complex organic haze. Water ice forms much of its crust, and a subsurface water ocean may exist. The Dragonfly rotorcraft mission is planned to investigate Titan’s prebiotic chemistry and surface environments.


## Uranus — the tilted ice giant

![Uranus — the tilted ice giant](<Uranus.jpg>)

Uranus appears cyan because methane absorbs red light in its upper atmosphere. Its extreme axial tilt means it rotates almost on its side, producing unusual seasons that last decades.

**Key facts:** **Mass:** 8.68 × 10²⁵ kg (14.5 Earth masses) · **Mean diameter:** 50,724 km · **Mean density:** 1.27 g/cm³. **Solar orbit:** 84 Earth years · **Rotation:** about 17.2 hours (retrograde in the usual convention).

**Why it matters:** Uranus is an ice giant with a hydrogen–helium atmosphere containing methane and a deep mantle rich in water-, ammonia-, and methane-bearing compounds. It has dark rings and a complex, tilted magnetic field.


## Uranus viewed from Miranda

![Uranus viewed from Miranda](<UranusfromMiranda.jpg>)

Miranda is one of Uranus’s small major moons, notable for enormous cliffs, grooved terrain, and patchwork regions that suggest a complex geological history. This scene is a simulation.

**Key facts:** **Miranda:** mean diameter about 472 km; mass ≈ 6.6 × 10¹⁹ kg; orbital period around Uranus ≈ 1.41 days. **Uranus:** solar orbital period ≈ 84 years.

**Why it matters:** Miranda is a mixture of water ice and rock. Its strange surface may reflect past tidal heating, tectonic disruption, or partial reassembly, though the exact history is unresolved. Voyager 2 remains the only spacecraft to have visited the Uranian system, in 1986.


## Venus — the runaway greenhouse world

![Venus — the runaway greenhouse world](<Venus.jpg>)

Venus is similar to Earth in size but has a radically different climate. Its dense carbon-dioxide atmosphere and sulphuric-acid clouds trap heat, making its surface hotter than Mercury’s despite being farther from the Sun.

**Key facts:** **Mass:** 4.87 × 10²⁴ kg (0.815 Earth masses) · **Mean diameter:** 12,104 km · **Surface gravity:** 8.87 m/s². **Solar orbit:** 224.7 Earth days · **Sidereal rotation:** 243 Earth days, retrograde.

**Why it matters:** Venus’s surface temperature is about 465 °C and pressure is roughly 92 times Earth’s sea-level pressure. Its rocky surface is shaped by volcanism and tectonics; whether significant volcanic activity continues today is an important research question.

### Accuracy and interpretation

- Numbers are rounded for readability. Masses, diameters, densities, temperatures, and orbital elements can differ slightly across references and may be revised as measurements improve.
- Some properties of distant dwarf planets and small moons are estimates rather than direct measurements.
- “Potentially habitable” or “ocean world” does **not** mean life has been found. No life beyond Earth has been confirmed.
